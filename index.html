Doxxing

What is doxxing and why is it dangerous?

Doxxing means publishing someone's private information — name, address, phone number, employer — without their consent, usually to invite harassment.

🏠

Home address exposure

A leaked address brings strangers to someone's door. People have had to leave their homes, change schools, and relocate because of this.

💼

Workplace targeting

Harassers contact employers directly. Careers end. People lose jobs they worked years to build because of coordinated complaint campaigns.

👨‍👩‍👧

Family information

Exposing relatives' details puts people who had nothing to do with any dispute in direct danger. Parents, siblings, children — all become targets.

⚖️

It's often illegal

Doxxing with intent to harass is a criminal offense in many countries and U.S. states. Charges range from stalking to cyberstalking to making threats.

📸

It doesn't come back

Once information is posted, it's cached, screenshotted, and mirrored. Deleting the original post does not remove the information from circulation.

🎯

Anyone can be a target

Doxxing is used against journalists, activists, gamers, students, and ordinary people. You don't have to be famous or controversial — a disagreement is enough.

Cyberbullying

Why bullying online causes real harm

Digital harassment doesn't turn off when you close the app. It follows people everywhere.

🧠

Mental health damage

Persistent bullying significantly increases rates of anxiety, depression, and PTSD. The research is consistent across age groups and platforms.

🚫

No safe space

Unlike in-person bullying, online harassment invades bedrooms, midnight, every notification. The target cannot escape by going home.

👥

Pile-ons multiply damage

Group harassment feels diffuse for participants — everyone does a little — but the target experiences all of it at once, every message, every notification.

📱

Screenshots are permanent

A cruel comment deleted after three minutes has already been screenshotted and can resurface years later. Nothing digital is truly temporary.

👀

Bystanders are participants

Watching without acting reads as approval. Liking, sharing, or staying silent all tell the bully the behavior is acceptable.

🎓

Academic and career impact

Students who are bullied online have significantly lower grades and higher dropout rates. Adults face job loss and professional damage.

Real Consequences

What harassment actually does to someone's life

These aren't edge cases. This is what sustained online harassment looks like in practice.

1

Immediate fear and hypervigilance

The target stops feeling safe anywhere — online or offline. Sleep disruption, constant anxiety, and difficulty concentrating follow immediately.

2

Social withdrawal

People delete accounts, go private, stop posting. They lose communities and connections they built over years to escape the harassment.

3

Professional damage

Employers find the harassment pile-on. Colleagues distance themselves. Opportunities disappear. Some people change careers entirely because their name becomes searchable as a harassment target.

4

Physical safety risk

Doxxing turns online harassment into physical danger. Swatting (false emergency calls to send police to someone's home) has resulted in deaths.

5

Long-term psychological effects

PTSD, depression, and social anxiety can persist for years after the harassment stops. For younger targets, it shapes how they relate to people and the internet for life.

Online Safety Tips

How to protect yourself

Most doxxing starts with information people share themselves. These habits significantly reduce your exposure.

Privacy settings

  • Set all social profiles to private
  • Review what old posts reveal
  • Remove location from photos before posting
  • Limit who can find you by email or phone
  • Audit third-party app access regularly

Passwords & accounts

  • Use a password manager — unique password per site
  • Enable two-factor authentication everywhere
  • Use a separate email for public accounts
  • Never reuse usernames across platforms
  • Check haveibeenpwned.com for breaches

What you share

  • Don't post your workplace or school publicly
  • Avoid sharing your general neighborhood
  • Be careful with real-time location sharing
  • Think before tagging others in photos
  • Use a P.O. Box for public-facing mail

If you're targeted

  • Document everything before reporting
  • Don't engage with harassers directly
  • Tell someone you trust immediately
  • Contact the platform's trust & safety team
  • File a police report if threats are made

If You're Being Bullied

Concrete steps you can take right now

Being targeted is disorienting. Here's what to do in order.

1

Save the evidence first

Screenshot everything with timestamps visible before you block or report. Platforms often delete flagged content — you need a record if it escalates.

2

Block the person

Block on every platform, not just the one where the harassment is happening. Don't engage — responses escalate things and give them more content.

3

Report to the platform

Use every available report mechanism: the post, the account, and any direct messages. Most platforms take doxxing and credible threats seriously.

4

Tell someone you trust

A parent, friend, teacher, or counselor. Isolation makes it worse. Someone who knows helps you think clearly and can support escalation if needed.

5

Escalate to authorities if there are threats

Explicit threats of physical harm warrant a police report. Bring your screenshots. Swatting calls and home address exposure are also police territory.

6

Take care of yourself

Being targeted online is traumatic. Take breaks from social media. Talk to someone. The harassment says nothing true about you.

Be Part of the Solution

Stand up without escalating

Intervening in harassment is worth doing. These approaches work without pouring fuel on the fire.

do this

Support the target privately

A direct message saying "I saw what happened, that's not okay, do you need help reporting it" costs you nothing and means more than any public statement.

do this

Report without amplifying

Report the content, then stop. Don't screenshot and reshare the attack as "raising awareness" — that extends its reach. Link to the target's response instead, if they made one.

do this

Redirect the conversation

In comment sections where a pile-on is forming, a calm statement that changes the subject is often enough to break the momentum. You don't have to argue with anyone.

avoid this

Don't counter-harass

Attacking the bully makes you a bully. It also gives them a reason to claim victimhood and recruit more people. Document and report — don't engage.

do this

Check in after

The acute phase of a harassment campaign ends, but the target is still dealing with it. Following up a week later matters.

do this

Model the behavior you want

How you talk about people online — in public and in private chats — sets norms in your community. Consistency matters more than any single intervention.

FAQ

Common questions

Answers to what people ask most.

Is doxxing always illegal?
Laws vary by country and state. In many U.S. states, doxxing with intent to harass is explicitly criminalized. Even where it isn't a specific statute, the resulting harassment, stalking, or threats usually are. The legal risk is real and growing as more jurisdictions pass specific legislation.
What if the information was already public?
Aggregating and republishing information — even individually public data — with the intent to facilitate harassment is considered doxxing. Compiling a person's address, employer, relatives, and daily routine from public sources and posting it as a package is a harm regardless of each piece's original availability.
What counts as cyberbullying?
Repeated, targeted harassment that causes distress — whether it's direct messages, public posts, exclusion campaigns, impersonation, or sharing embarrassing content without consent. A single heated argument isn't cyberbullying. A sustained pattern aimed at making someone's online life miserable is.
Can I get information removed if I've been doxxed?
Yes, partially. File a report with each platform hosting the information. Submit removal requests to data broker sites — services like DeleteMe automate this process. Contact Google to request removal from search results. Full removal is very hard; speed matters because the longer it's up, the more it spreads.
What do I do if someone I know is being bullied?
Tell them you've seen it and ask if they want help. Don't share or engage with the harassment content. If they're a minor and there are threats, tell a trusted adult or contact the school. See the What to Do If You're Being Bullied section above for specific steps you can help them take.
What if the platform won't do anything?
Escalate. File a report with your country's cybercrime unit or internet safety agency. In the U.S., the FBI's IC3 (ic3.gov) handles internet crime complaints. Keep documentation of everything including the platform's inaction, as this supports future legal action.
